## Summary

Pairs of phrases built alike, word for word, and how to use them to read.

## Explanation

**What it is.** Parallelism (對偶 duì'ǒu, 對仗 duìzhàng, 排比 páibǐ for a longer series) is
the matching of two or more phrases that have the same structure: noun against noun, verb
against verb, often with the same number of characters. It is the most important device of
literary Chinese prose, and you have already met it in almost every unit:

- in comparisons (老年人如…，少年人如…);
- in arguments (用…則…；用…則…);
- in lists of actions (以…箴其民，以…儆其軍，以…訓其國人).

**How to use it.** Parallelism is a reading tool as well as an ornament.

1. **Find the frame.** Look for the words that repeat (如, 以…之, 則, 已/又) and the slots
   between them. Write the halves one above the other.
2. **Divide.** Each half is a clause: put a comma or semicolon between them. A pair inside a
   longer pair is divided by commas; the larger halves by a semicolon.
3. **Read across.** Words in the same slot have the same function and often related
   meanings. If you know one, you can often guess the other: in 振刷整頓，斟酌通變, the four
   verbs are all "reform" words.
4. **Read the contrast.** The point is often in the slots that differ: 漢以前 against 近世,
   易 against 難.

**Loose and strict.** In verse and in parallel prose (駢文), the pairs match exactly in length,
word class and even tone. In ordinary prose they are looser: two halves may differ in length
but keep the same structure (囂訟者，已… / 傾心彼族者，又…). Look for matching **structure**,
not just matching length.

**Series.** Three or more parallel phrases form a series (排比). A series often ends with a
summary: 此老年與少年性格不同之大略也.
