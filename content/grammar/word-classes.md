## Summary

The same word can work as a noun, verb or adjective; its position in the sentence tells you which.

## Explanation

**Characters and words.** Most words in literary Chinese are a single character, and so
a single syllable: 日 "sun", 光 "light", 借 "to borrow". There are also words of two or more
characters (行星 "planet", 地球 "earth"), especially in newer vocabulary and in names.

**No endings.** Words do not change their form. There is nothing like English *-s*,
*-ed* or *-ly* to show plural, tense or word class. A word's job in the sentence is shown by:

- **position**: what comes before and after it;
- **function words** (particles such as 也, 之, 而), which you will meet unit by unit.

**The same word in different jobs.** Many words that are usually nouns can also work as
verbs or adverbs, depending on where they stand:

| Word | Usual class | In the passage | Job here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 夜 | noun "night" | 夜見西方 | before a verb: "at night" |
| 背 | noun "back" | 背日一面無光 | before an object: "to turn one's back on" |
| 日 | noun "sun" | 每一月三十日 | after a number: "day" |

**A reading strategy.** When a word seems not to fit, ask what job its position calls for:

- before a verb: often *how* or *when* (an adverb or time expression);
- directly before a noun that is its object: a verb;
- before a noun it describes: an adjective, a modifier.

**Reading may change too.** Sometimes a change of job or meaning also changes the
pronunciation: 見 jiàn "to see" but xiàn "to appear"; 朝 zhāo "morning" but cháo "court,
dynasty". The glosses always give the reading for the context.

**Word-class terms.** Chinese grammarians took over European word classes at the end of the
19th century. The nine classes 名、代、動、靜、狀、介、連、助、嘆 come from 《馬氏文通》 (1898). This course uses the English terms, defined in
[Grammatical terms](../toolkit/t07-grammatical-terms.md).
