## Summary

Strategies for finding sentence and clause breaks in text printed without punctuation: particles, parallelism, quotation, lists and layout.

## Explanation

**Why it matters.** Most texts of this period were printed without modern punctuation. Some
have small circles or dots beside the characters (句讀 marks: 。 for 句 jù "sentence", 、 for
讀 dòu "pause"), added by the printer or a reader; many have nothing at all. Modern editions
add punctuation, but that is an **editor's interpretation**. As a researcher you will need
to check it, and often to do without it.

**A checklist.** Work through the text looking for these signals:

1. **Final particles**: 也, 矣, 焉, 哉, 乎, 耶, 歟, 耳, 而已. A break usually follows them.
2. **Initial words**: 夫, 蓋, 故, 是以, 然, 然則, 若, 苟, 雖, 且, 惟, 今, 昔. A break usually comes **before** them.
3. **Linking words inside the sentence**: 則, 而, 以 often begin the second part of a clause; a comma often comes before them.
4. **Quotation**: 曰, 云, 謂 introduce speech or a quotation: colon after them.
5. **Parallelism**: phrases of the same length and structure (以…箴其民，以…儆其軍) are separate clauses. Count characters: four and six are especially common.
6. **Lists**: a run of names or nouns (齊晉秦宋) is a list, divided with 、.
7. **Layout**: a new column, an article number, a gap or a raised character (see
   [Elevation](elevation.md)) often marks a new unit.

**Then check by meaning.** Divide provisionally, translate each part, and see whether the
grammar works: does each clause have what it needs (a verb, its object)? If a clause ends
in the middle of a verb–object pair or a 之-phrase, move the break.

**Typical mistakes.**

- Breaking inside a fixed expression: 所以, 而已, 以至, 何為.
- Joining characters that only look like a word: 等 and 於 in 大憲等於道光十九年 (see
  [Opium and silver](../units/u06-prepositions/u06-p03-opium-and-silver.md)).
- Taking a list of single-character names for a sentence.
