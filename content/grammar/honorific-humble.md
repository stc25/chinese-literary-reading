## Summary

Words that raise the person addressed and lower oneself.

## Explanation

**The principle.** Polite literary Chinese avoids plain "I" and "you". The writer lowers himself
and everything that is his; he raises the other person and everything that is theirs. The degree
depends on the relationship: a commoner to a minister, a son to his parents, friend to friend.

**Talking about oneself.**

| Form | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| given name (文, 國藩) | I | to superiors and elders |
| 男 | your son | to parents |
| 弟 | your younger brother | between friends, whatever their ages |
| 僕, 愚 | your servant, the foolish one | humble "I" |
| 竊 | "I venture to" (lit. "stealthily") | before a statement of opinion |
| 伏 | "prostrate" | before 維, 祈, 乞 in petitions |
| 管見, 愚見 | my narrow / foolish view | |
| 拙作, 拙著 | my clumsy work | |
| 敝 | my humble (敝處, 敝友) | |
| 家 | my (elder relatives): 家父, 家母, 家兄 | |
| 舍 | my (younger relatives): 舍弟, 舍妹 | |

**Talking about the other.**

| Form | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| 足下, 閣下 | you (lit. "at your feet", "below your pavilion") | equals, superiors |
| 兄, 大兄, 仁兄 | you, elder brother | between friends |
| 中堂, 大人, 鈞座 | Your Excellency | to high officials |
| 令 | your (relatives): 令尊 "your father", 令堂 "your mother", 令弟 "your younger brother" | |
| 尊 | your (honoured): 尊體 "your health", 尊意 "your view" | |
| 貴 | your (honoured): 貴國, 貴姓, 貴處 | |
| 台 | your (between equals): 台鑒, 台端 | |
| 鈞 | your (to superiors): 鈞座, 鈞鑒, 鈞綏 | |
| 駕 | you (lit. "your carriage"): 駕遊 "you travel" | |
| 垂, 賜 | (you) deign to: 垂鑒 "deign to read", 賜覆 "deign to reply" | |

**Watch the pairs.** 令弟 "your younger brother" ↔ 舍弟 "my younger brother"; 令尊 "your father"
↔ 家父 "my father"; 尊意 "your opinion" ↔ 愚見 "my opinion"; 貴國 "your country" ↔ 敝國 "my
country".

**Reading tips.**

- 弟 at the start of a sentence in a letter between friends is almost always "I", not "younger
  brother". Check whether a 令 or 舍 is in front of it.
- A sentence that begins with 竊 or 伏維 expresses the writer's own view, however modestly put.
- Honorifics are not personal flattery but the grammar of the relationship. A missing honorific is
  noticeable; Zeng Guofan's 爾 "you" to his sons is normal only because he writes downwards.
