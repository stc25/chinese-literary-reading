## Summary

Between a modifier and a noun, 之 works like English "of" or "'s": X之Y is "the Y of X".

## Explanation

In the pattern **X 之 Y**, Y is a noun and X describes it. X can be:

- a person or thing that Y belongs to: 一家之私產 "the private property of one family";
- a place or time: 秋後之柳 "the willow after autumn";
- a whole clause: 老年與少年性格不同之大略 "the outline of (how) the old and the young differ in character".

The modifier always comes **before** the noun, so English usually reverses the order:
read Y first, then "of X".

**How to recognise it.** Look at what comes right after 之. If it is a noun that ends the
phrase, as in 歐西人之言 "the words of Europeans", 之 is joining a modifier to it.
Compare the two other common uses of 之:

| Use | What follows 之 | Example |
|---|---|---|
| joins modifier and noun (this page) | a noun, ending the phrase | 秋後之柳 "the willow after autumn" |
| between subject and verb | a verb, often with an object | 日本人之稱我中國也 "the Japanese calling our China" |
| object pronoun | nothing: the clause ends | 人固有之 "people indeed have it" |

**When 之 is left out.** Short, close-knit modifiers often join the noun directly:
少年中國 "young China", 老年人 "old people". 之 is more likely when the modifier is long,
or when the writer wants an even rhythm.
