## Summary

Placed between a subject and its verb, 之 turns the clause into a noun phrase, which can then be a topic, subject or object.

## Explanation

Take a simple clause, 日本人稱我中國 "the Japanese call our China". Put 之 between the
subject (日本人) and the verb (稱), and the clause becomes a **noun phrase**:
日本人之稱我中國 "the Japanese calling our China". The noun phrase can then do whatever a noun does:

- be the **topic** of a sentence, usually closed by 也:
  日本人之稱我中國也，一則曰老大帝國 "as for the Japanese speaking of our China, first they call it 'the old empire'";
- be the **object** of a verb: 老年人如死海之瀦爲澤 "the old are like the Dead Sea's pooling
  into a marsh".

**Translating it.** A literal rendering with "-ing" ("the Japanese calling…") shows the
structure but is clumsy. In a translation, use whatever suits the context:

- "when X does Y": "when the Japanese speak of our China…";
- "the way X does Y", "the fact that X does Y";
- a relative clause: "the Dead Sea, which has pooled into a marsh".

**How to recognise it.** After 之 comes a **verb**, often followed by an object or
complement (稱我中國, 瀦爲澤, 初發源). When 之 joins a modifier to a noun, a noun follows
instead and ends the phrase. See [之 joining modifier and noun](zhi-attributive.md).
