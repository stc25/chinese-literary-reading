## Summary

Prose built from phrases of four and six characters (四六).

## Explanation

**Rhythm.** Literary Chinese prose has a strong tendency to move in units of **four
characters**, often paired, and in longer formal writing in alternating units of four and
six. The most formal style, parallel prose (駢文 piánwén, also called 四六文 "four-six
prose"), was built entirely of matched four- and six-character lines, with allusions in
almost every phrase. By 1900 few people wrote whole texts in it, but prefaces, memorial
addresses (祭文), congratulatory messages and formal letters still used it, and its rhythm runs
through ordinary prose too.

**Kinds of four-character unit.**

| Kind | Example |
|---|---|
| a fixed expression (成語) | 良辰美景 "a fine day and a beautiful scene" |
| two parallel pairs | 委心任運 = 委心 + 任運 "give up the heart, trust to fate" |
| two near-synonyms doubled | 振刷整頓 "reform and reorganise" |
| two nouns joined | 精理微言 "subtle principles, profound words" |
| a four-character modifier with 之 | 挾山超海之意氣 "the spirit of carrying mountains and leaping seas" |

**Using rhythm to read.**

- In unpunctuated text, try dividing into fours: if it makes sense, you have probably found the breaks.
- A four-character unit usually divides **2 + 2**: 委心｜任運, 振刷｜整頓. Read each half as a word or a verb–object pair.
- When the rhythm breaks (a five- or seven-character clause after a run of fours), something important is often being said: the conclusion, or the point the writer wants you to notice.

**Six characters.** A six-character line usually divides 3 + 3 or 2 + 2 + 2, or is a
four-character unit with a function word on each side (以…之…).
