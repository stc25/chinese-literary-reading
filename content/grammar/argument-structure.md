## Summary

Words that open, develop and conclude an argument, and the usual shape of a refutation.

## Explanation

**Signposts.** Classical argument has few paragraph breaks and no headings. Its structure is
carried by short words at the start of a clause. Learn to spot them first; they tell you what
the next sentence is for.

| Word | Pinyin | Function | Translate as |
|---|---|---|---|
| 夫 | fú | opens a general statement or a new step | "now", "as for", or leave untranslated |
| 蓋 | gài | gives the reason or the underlying fact; also "probably" | "for", "the fact is"; "probably" |
| 然 / 然而 | rán / rán'ér | turns against what precedes | "but", "and yet" |
| 雖然 | suīrán | concedes, then turns | "even so", "and yet" |
| 然則 | ránzé | draws a conclusion from what precedes | "that being so", "then" |
| 是故 / 故 | shìgù / gù | states a consequence | "therefore" |
| 且 | qiě | adds a further point | "moreover" |
| 夫而後 | fú ér hòu | states the condition that must come first | "only then" |
| 若 / 若云 | ruò / ruò yún | puts a hypothesis or an opponent's claim | "if", "if it is said" |

Do not confuse 夫 fú (sentence-initial, "now") with 夫 fū "man, husband", or the final particle
夫 fú "alas" at the end of an exclamation.

**蓋 has two uses.** At the start of a sentence that explains, it means "for, the fact is"
(蓋人之由散入群，原為安利). Before a verb in a statement of fact, it means "probably"
(蓋昉於海格爾氏). The first is far more common in argument.

**The shape of a refutation.** Classical writers rarely attack directly. The usual sequence is:

1. **State or grant the other view.** 可謂……矣 "may be called …", 吾不謂……非也 "I do not say
   … is wrong".
2. **Turn.** 然, 然而, 雖然, 顧, 但.
3. **Name the fault.** 則有……之病 "then there is the fault of …", 彼不悟…… "what they fail to see
   is …".
4. **Give the reason.** 蓋……; 夫既……則…… "now, since …, then …".
5. **Conclude.** 然則……; 此其……所以…… "this is why …"; 故曰…… "hence it is said".

**Chains.** A sequence of 則 clauses, each beginning with the end of the last, makes a causal
chain: 自由侈則侵，侵則爭，爭則群渙. This figure is called 頂真 dǐngzhēn. When you meet one,
the last link is usually the point.

**Rhetorical questions and dilemmas.** An argument often ends with a question whose answer is
obvious (豈……？, ……乎？) or with a choice between two answers: 果……邪？其抑……邪？ "is it really
…? or is it rather …?". See [Rhetorical questions](rhetorical-questions.md).
