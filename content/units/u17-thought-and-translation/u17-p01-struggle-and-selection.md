## Introduction

Thomas Henry Huxley's lecture *Evolution and Ethics* (1893) argued that human ethics must
resist the "cosmic process" of struggle and survival. Yan Fu translated it, freely, as
《天演論》 "On Evolution". The book appeared in 1898, three years after the defeat by Japan,
and its message, as Chinese readers took it, was alarming: nations, like species, struggle
for survival, and the weak perish. Phrases from it, 物競天擇 and 適者生存, became slogans.
The young Hu Shi 胡適 took his given name from 適.

This passage takes the opening sentences of the first chapter and the paragraph in which
Yan introduces his key terms. You met Yan's principles of translation, 信、達、雅, in
[Faithful, fluent, elegant](../u12-parallelism-and-allusion/u12-p01-faithful-fluent-elegant.md).
Watch how he defines each new term with ……者，……也 and sets them in the old pair 體 and 用.

## Text

赫胥黎獨處一室之中，在英倫之南，背山而面野，檻外諸境，歷歷如在几下。乃懸想二千年前，當羅馬大將愷徹未到時，此間有何景物。……

故知不變一言，決非天運。而悠久成物之理，轉在變動不居之中。是當前之所見，經廿年卅年而革焉可也，更二萬年三萬年而革亦可也。特據前事推將來，為變方長，未知所極而已。雖然，天運變矣，而有不變者行乎其中。不變惟何？是名天演。以天演為體，而其用有二：曰物競，曰天擇。此萬物莫不然，而於有生之類為尤著。物競者，物爭自存也。以一物以與物物爭，或存或亡，而其效則歸於天擇。天擇者，物爭焉而獨存。則其存也，必有其所以存，必其所得於天之分，自致一己之能，與其所遭值之時與地，及凡周身以外之物力，有其相謀相劑者焉。夫而後獨免於亡，而足以自立也。而自其效觀之，若是物特為天之所厚而擇焉以存也者，夫是之謂天擇。天擇者，擇於自然，雖擇而莫之擇，猶物競之無所爭，而實天下之至爭也。斯賓塞爾曰：「天擇者，存其最宜者也。」夫物既爭存矣，而天又從其爭之後而擇之，一爭一擇，而變化之事出矣。

## Translation

Huxley is sitting alone in a room in the south of England, with hills behind and fields in front; the scenes beyond the railing are as clear as if they lay below his desk. He then imagines what this place looked like two thousand years ago, before the Roman general Caesar came. ……

Hence we know that "unchanging" is by no means the way of heaven's course, and that the principle by which things endure and take shape lies, on the contrary, in ceaseless change. What we see before us may well be transformed in twenty or thirty years, or again in twenty or thirty thousand. It is only that, inferring the future from past events, change has far still to run, and we do not know where it will end. And yet, though heaven's course changes, something unchanging operates within it. What is this unchanging thing? It is called evolution (天演). Evolution is the substance, and it has two functions: the struggle of things (物競) and the selection of heaven (天擇). This is so of all things, and most evident among living things. The struggle of things is things striving to preserve themselves. When one thing contends with thing after thing, it either survives or perishes, and the result comes down to the selection of heaven. The selection of heaven is when things contend and one alone survives. Then there must be a reason for its survival: its endowment from heaven, the abilities it attains by itself, the time and place it meets with, and all the forces of things outside its body must have worked together and complemented one another. Only then does it alone escape perishing and become able to stand on its own. And looking at it from the result, it is as if this thing had been specially favoured by heaven and selected to survive: this is what is called the selection of heaven. The selection of heaven is selection by nature: it selects, yet no one selects; just as the struggle of things seems to be no struggle, and is in truth the fiercest struggle in the world. Spencer said: "The selection of heaven is the preservation of the fittest." Since things struggle to survive, and heaven then selects among them after their struggle, with each struggle and each selection, change comes about.

## Notes

**Huxley in the third person.** Huxley's lecture opens in the first person: "It may be safely
assumed that, two thousand years ago, before Caesar set foot in southern Britain, the whole
countryside visible from the windows of the room in which I write, was in what is called 'the
state of nature.'" Yan Fu turns "I" into 赫胥黎 and sets the scene like the opening of a
Chinese essay or a story: 赫胥黎獨處一室之中. The translator speaks about the author instead of
through him.

**Defining the new terms.** Each new term is introduced with a name and then defined with the
classical definition pattern ……者，……也 (see [者…也](../../grammar/zhe-ye-definition.md)):

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| 物競 | 物競者，物爭自存也。 |
| 天擇 | 天擇者，物爭焉而獨存。 |
| 天擇 (Spencer) | 天擇者，存其最宜者也。 |

The last is Spencer's "survival of the fittest". 最宜 "the most suitable" was later replaced by
適者生存 "the fit survive", from which Hu Shi took his name.

**Old philosophy for new science.** Yan does not borrow new words; he makes them from old ones.
天 "heaven" in 天演 and 天擇 replaces "nature" and "the cosmic process". The pair 體 "substance"
and 用 "function", from Neo-Confucian philosophy (and the reform slogan 中學為體，西學為用),
structures the whole idea: evolution is the substance; struggle and selection are how it works.
Huxley has nothing like this. A Chinese reader met Darwin through Chinese categories. See
[Philosophical vocabulary](../../grammar/philosophical-terms.md).

**變動不居.** "Changing and moving, never resting" is a phrase from the 繫辭 commentary to the
*Book of Changes* (變動不居，周流六虛). Yan's evolution sounds like the *Changes*.

**Later terms.** Yan's 天演 lost out to the Japanese 進化, and 天擇 to 自然選擇 or 自然淘汰;
物競 survives in the set phrase 物競天擇. Compare Wang Guowei's judgement in
[Words from Japan](../u16-the-press-and-new-vocabulary/u16-p02-words-from-japan.md).

**以一物以與物物爭.** The doubled 以 is odd, but it is in the edition. Read it as "when one thing
contends with other things".

## Exercises

### 1. choice

What does Yan Fu call the "substance" (體) of the process?

### 2. short-answer

How does Yan Fu define 物競?

**Answer:** 物競者，物爭自存也: the struggle of things is things striving to preserve themselves.

### 3. short-answer

Huxley writes "the room in which I write". How does Yan Fu change this, and what is the effect?

**Answer:** He writes 赫胥黎獨處一室之中, putting Huxley in the third person. The translator describes the author, like the narrator of a Chinese essay, instead of speaking as him.

### 4. translate

Translate 天擇者，擇於自然，雖擇而莫之擇。

**Answer:** The selection of heaven is selection by nature: it selects, yet no one selects.
